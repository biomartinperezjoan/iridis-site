## The situation

A 2025 paper in *Nature Neuroscience* challenged one of the most widely cited ideas in dopamine research: that brief, subsecond dopamine spikes control the speed and force of individual movements in real time. The findings had clear implications for Parkinson's disease research and treatment, but the study design was technically dense, involving optogenetics, fluorescent dopamine sensors, and a lever-press paradigm in mice.

The piece needed to serve a scientifically literate audience that was not necessarily familiar with dopamine pharmacology, motor circuit anatomy, or the distinction between phasic and tonic signalling.

## What we did

We read the original paper and identified the single conceptual shift at its core: dopamine does not act as a real-time dial for movement intensity, it sets a permissive background level that allows motor circuits to function.

From there, we built the summary around the questions a reader unfamiliar with the field would ask first: what was the prevailing assumption, how was it tested, and what do the results actually change? We explained the methods in plain language without stripping out the scientific logic, and framed the Parkinson's disease implications carefully, acknowledging what the study does and does not show.

## The outcome

An 800-word research summary that holds up to a clinician's reading while remaining accessible to a broader scientific audience. The piece is reproduced in full below.

## Does Dopamine Control How Fast We Move? New Research Challenges a Key Assumption

<p class="case-sample-ref">Liu, H., Melani, R., Maltese, M., Taniguchi, J., et al. (2025). Subsecond dopamine fluctuations do not specify the vigor of ongoing actions. <em>Nature Neuroscience</em>, 28, 2432–2438. DOI: 10.1038/s41593-025-02102-1</p>

### Background

Dopamine is a chemical messenger in the brain that most people associate with pleasure and reward. In neuroscience, its role is understood more precisely: dopamine neurons fire in response to unexpected outcomes, helping the brain learn from experience by signalling when something turned out better or worse than expected. This process — known as reward prediction error signalling — is fundamental to how we form habits, make decisions, and adjust behaviour over time.

Dopamine is also known to be essential for movement. Patients with Parkinson's disease lose dopamine-producing neurons in a brain region called the substantia nigra, and the result is bradykinesia, a slowing and reduction in the size of voluntary movements. Levodopa, the main treatment for Parkinson's, works by boosting dopamine levels and partially restoring normal movement.

A popular hypothesis in neuroscience holds that dopamine does more than just enable movement in a general sense — it may fine-tune the speed and force of each individual movement in real time. According to this idea, brief spikes in dopamine that last less than a second (called "phasic" or "subsecond" transients) function as a live gain signal, amplifying the brain's motor commands moment by moment. This study sets out to test that idea directly.

### Methods and results

Researchers at NYU trained mice to press a small lever with one forelimb in exchange for water rewards. The task was designed to measure movement vigor precisely, tracking both the peak speed and the amplitude of each press. This made it possible to ask whether changes in dopamine levels change how vigorously animals move.

First, the team confirmed that dopamine matters for the task: destroying dopamine neurons on the side of the brain opposite to the pressing forelimb severely reduced movement speed and size. Administering levodopa restored vigorous pressing, confirming both the task design and its relevance to Parkinson's disease.

Next, they measured dopamine levels in real time during pressing, using a fluorescent sensor (rDA1m) implanted in the striatum, the brain region that receives dopamine signals and controls movement. Surprisingly, dopamine did not rise reliably before or during movements. In the dorsolateral striatum, dopamine levels dipped slightly as movements peaked. Across thousands of individual presses, there was little to no correlation between the size of a dopamine fluctuation and how fast or forceful a given press was.

To assess whether phasic dopamine fluctuations are necessary for vigorous movement, the researchers used levodopa to restore dopamine in animals whose dopamine neurons had been destroyed. Crucially, levodopa produced a slow, sustained rise in dopamine — without reinstating the rapid subsecond transients normally present in healthy animals. Despite the absence of phasic signals, mice regained their ability to press vigorously. This showed that a steady baseline level of dopamine, not brief spikes, is sufficient for vigorous movement.

Finally, the team used light-based tools (optogenetics) to directly switch dopamine neurons on or off for precisely timed intervals during the task. Switching them off for 2 seconds at the start of a trial did not slow movements down. Switching them on — producing a dopamine spike equivalent to or larger than what naturally occurs — did not speed movements up either. These results held across hundreds of trials and multiple animals.

### Conclusion

This study provides robust evidence that brief, subsecond dopamine spikes are not the signal that decides how fast or forceful an individual movement is. Rather than acting as a real-time dial controlling movement intensity, dopamine appears to set a permissive background level that allows the brain's motor circuits to function normally. When that background level is lost, as in Parkinson's disease, movements become slow and small. When it is restored, as with levodopa, vigorous movement returns — even without the rapid fluctuations that dopamine normally produces.

This does not mean that phasic dopamine transients are unimportant. The researchers suggest they likely play a role in motor learning: by briefly boosting dopamine at moments when a movement is particularly well-executed, the brain can gradually reinforce the neural patterns responsible for that movement over many repetitions. This is consistent with dopamine's broader role in reinforcement learning and helps explain why the same system underlies both reward-based learning and motor control.

For Parkinson's disease research, the findings suggest that the therapeutic benefit of levodopa may come from restoring a steady dopamine tone rather than from recreating the dynamic, pulsatile signalling of healthy neurons. This distinction could inform the development of more effective and better-tolerated treatments.
